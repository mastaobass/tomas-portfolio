## Context

Policy management is where risk appetite becomes executable rules. Analysts encode who to block, who to allow, and under what conditions. On a platform that grew past a million transactions a day, a slow or illegible rules surface is not a convenience problem. It is a production risk.

The old surface was missing OR logic and versioning. Triggers were hard to reason about. Changes that should have been staged went live because there was no safe middle ground. Design arrived late on more than one cycle: product leadership shifted three times, scope grew without full validation, and the bias was toward shipping over usability.

## The real constraint

A bad rule ships into live traffic. The design problem is not fewer form fields. It is legibility of boolean logic plus a path to change policy without betting the day on a single publish.

That constraint shaped every version. Features that made rules easier to write but harder to stage were incomplete. Features that added control but buried the logic failed the people who had to defend a decision later.

## Three versions, one surface

The work shipped in three versions rather than one rewrite.

**Version 1** added OR logic, traffic segmentation, and scheduled policy changes. The goal was simpler rules and planned updates instead of emergency publishes.

**Version 2** expanded event types, merged legacy code bases, removed policy sets, and renamed triggers to segments. The mental model tightened: a segment is a named slice of traffic, not a hidden trigger the system fires for reasons no one can explain.

**Version 3** brought versioning back with policy sets used for staging, cleaned up scheduling, and laid groundwork for testing rules against different data sets. Control returned without undoing the clarity segments had bought.

## What we kept and what we cut

The useful work was deciding which half of the old model deserved to survive.

Segments stayed. They made rule targeting readable. OR logic stayed. It cut the gymnastics required to express common policies. Versioning came back after a period without it, because staging turned out to be non-negotiable once volume and risk appetite both rose.

Policy sets were removed in Version 2 and restored in Version 3 with a clearer job: staging and controlled rollout, not a second place to hide logic. Triggers as a concept did not survive the rename. If analysts could not say what fired and why, the label was wrong.

I had designed pieces of this surface earlier in the Kount 360 lifecycle. Deciding which of my own prior decisions still held was part of the job.

## Evidence

What I can stand behind: the platform scaled from roughly 100,000 transactions a day to over a million during this work. OR logic and the segment model reduced the steps required to create a rule. Internal measurement showed task completion for new policies and adoption of the new model moving in the right direction. Analyst language shifted from triggers being confusing to segments clarifying what fired.

I am not attaching a single hero percentage to this study. The evidence is the sequence of decisions under real volume and real leadership change, not a number I would have to invent.

## Close

What shipped was a rules surface fraud teams could write against and stage against while the platform crossed a million transactions a day. The product did not invent policy management. It made complex boolean policy legible enough that the people who own fraud outcomes could change it without treating every publish as a leap of faith.
